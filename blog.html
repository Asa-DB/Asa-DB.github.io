Technology and Mental Health
APRIL 10, 2026 | philosophy and technology
NOTE: I had initially written this for my English class.

There is a widespread belief that modern life is experiencing a crisis caused by technology, particularly smartphones. They are often blamed for distraction, declining attention spans, and a general sense of dissatisfaction.

The solution that follows from this framing is usually behavioral: reduce screen time, remove apps, limit access, restore control.

But this assumes that the problem originates in behavior itself.

A more careful interpretation suggests that technology is not the source of the condition, but the medium through which an existing condition becomes visible.

In Technopoly, Neil Postman argues that technological societies undergo a shift in which tools stop serving cultural values and instead begin to define them. When this happens, efficiency, measurement, and optimization begin to replace older frameworks of meaning. What can be quantified becomes what is considered real.

This creates a subtle but important distortion. Value becomes increasingly tied to what is measurable, while less quantifiable aspects of life [purpose, meaning, and fulfillment] lose their structural support.

This concern is not new.

In The Gay Science, Friedrich Nietzsche describes the collapse of shared metaphysical grounding in his (infamous) declaration that "God is dead." This is not simply a statement about religion, but about the weakening of inherited structures that once provided meaning, interpretation, and direction. When those structures dissolve, individuals are left with the task of generating meaning without a stable foundation.

Later, in Thus Spoke Zarathustra, Friedrich Nietzsche introduces the idea that humanity is in a transitional state, no longer anchored by traditional value systems, but not yet capable of fully creating new ones. The result is a tension between inherited meaning and self-generated meaning, often unresolved at the level of everyday life.

Within this context, technology does not create emptiness, but it becomes one of the primary ways that emptiness is managed.

Smartphones, in particular, function as high-efficiency systems of engagement. They provide immediate stimulation, social feedback, and structured interaction. But these features are not inherently problematic. They become significant because they operate within a broader environment where alternative sources of meaning are often weaker or less accessible.

It is important to note that empirical research does not strongly support the idea that smartphones, in themselves, are a primary cause of mental health decline. Large-scale studies in digital psychology suggest that correlations between screen time and well-being are generally small or inconsistent, and are often mediated by external factors such as social environment, loneliness, or pre-existing mental health conditions.

This distinction matters.

If smartphones were the root cause, then reduction alone would produce consistent improvement. However, in many cases, limiting use does not resolve underlying dissatisfaction. It merely removes a dominant coping mechanism without replacing it.

This is why interventions focused purely on restriction, screen time limits, digital detoxes, and app blocking, often produce limited long-term change. They address behavior without addressing the conditions that make the behavior compelling.

The deeper issue is structural.

Modern societies are highly optimized for measurable outputs: productivity, engagement, efficiency, and performance. These metrics provide clarity, but they do not provide meaning. They describe activity without defining purpose.

As a result, individuals often find themselves in environments where action is constant but direction is unclear. In such conditions, highly accessible forms of stimulation naturally become default responses.

The question, then, is not why people use their phones.

It is what else, in their environment, competes with it.

Instead of a problem of overuse, it becomes a problem of under supplied meaning.

A fulfillment problem.

And until that structural gap is addressed, technological restriction alone will remain insufficient, not because it is ineffective, but because it is incomplete.

I am a Chainsaw Man
APRIL 5, 2026 | media and personal reflections
NOTE: I had originally written this on March 26, 2026, two days after the Chainsaw Man manga ended. I have since then tweaked it a bit, but the core message stays the same.

Chainsaw Man was not just something I read. It was something that reached me at a time when I did not feel like I belonged anywhere.

For a long time, I carried a sense of shame that made it difficult to exist comfortably in the world. It felt as though there was no place for me, that there was no why I could be accepted without resistance. This did numbers to me, and even the small things felt heavy.

Then I saw Denji.

There was nothing traditionally admirable about him. His goals were simple, even crude at times. He could be selfish, impulsive, and shortsighted. But he was real in a way that I had not seen before. He struggled, got discouraged, and yet he continued. Not because he had some grandiose moral compass, but because he kept choosing to move forward, even in small ways.

He got back up from it, and that was something I had not learned how to do.

Seeing that changed me. For the first time, I felt that there might be a place for someone like me. Not an idealized version of myself, but the version that already existed.

Chainsaw Man was more than just a story to me. It was a source of guidance. Something I returned to when I needed direction, or at least hope that direction was possible.

I trusted Tatsuki Fujimoto because of that. I believed that something which had carried me so far would continue to do so.

When the story moved forward, I expected it to resolve the things that still felt unfinished, both within the narrative and within myself.

Instead, the ending felt abrupt. Not because of what it aimed for, but because of how much it left behind. There was more that could have been explored, more that could have been understood.

And yet, with time, that feeling began to change.

Perhaps it was never meant to carry me all the way. Perhaps it was only meant to bring me to a point where I could begin to carry myself.

I am not the same person who first found that story. I am no longer someone who cannot get back up. That process did not happen all at once. It was gradual. Built over time, through repeated effort, through moments of failure and recovery.

What I once looked for in something external, I now try to build internally.

The story did not need to be perfect to have meaning. It did not need to go on forever for it to have mattered. It served its role, and in doing so, it left something behind that did not disappear when it ended.

In a way, that is enough.

If there is anything left to take from it now, it is this: I no longer need to rely on something else to continue forward.

That responsibility belongs to me.

And in that sense, whatever I saw in Denji was never entirely separate from myself to begin with. It's time to be my own Chainsaw Man.

Life's Sculpture
APRIL 5, 2026 | philosophy notes

There is a common belief that happiness is a choice. It is often said with good intentions, but it is a flawed concept. It assumes that your feelings can be commanded, that the mind will simply follow instruction.

That is not how people work.

A person does not choose sadness at the moment it appears, just as they do not choose frustration when faced with struggle. Emotions arise on their own. They are shaped by circumstance, memory, and perception. Ignoring this leads to a shallow understanding of experience.

At the same time, stopping there would be another mistake.

While feeling is not fully within one's control, response remains open. Between emotion and action there is a space. It is small and often missed, even by myself, but it exists. Within that space, a person can begin to shape themselves.

To acknowledge a feeling without immediately obeying it takes a kind of discipline. It isn't a harsh or suppressive kind of discipline, but more akin to a stable self awareness. A person can feel anger and still choose restraint. They can feel unmotivated and still continue. They can feel uncertain and still move forward. This way, emotion is not rejected, but it is not given full authority either.

Over time, this becomes a skill.

It is easy to imagine that a good life is one without struggle, but that is rarely true. Difficulty is not an interruption of life. It is part of its structure. What changes is not the presence of hardship, but the way it is met.

Each moment of recognition slowly sculpts something grander. You begin to notice a spiral before it deepens. Pausing instead of reacting. Choosing a better response, even when it feels unnatural. These moments do not create perfection. They create resilience. The ability to continue without needing everything to be ideal.

Life is not found fully formed, but shaped gradually, like a kind of sculpture. Each response leaves its own mark. Each decision refines or distorts what is being made. Life's Sculpture is not carved in a single moment, but through repeated acts of awareness and choice.

Happiness, then, does not work well as a direct goal. It is too dependent and too unstable when chased on its own. It appears more often as a result of something else. A life that feels directed, intentional, and in some sense chosen. (Although some people prefer to live with the flow, this too can be a form of direction. To accept life as it comes is still a choice, and in that sense, it remains part of a life that is chosen.)

This does not mean a person will always succeed. There will be times when awareness fails, when emotion takes over, when old patterns return without warning. These moments do not erase progress. They are part of the process of shaping Life's Sculpture.

What matters isn't constant control, but gradual change. To recover a little faster. To see a little more clearly. To choose, even if only slightly, more often than before.

In the end, a person may not be able to decide what they feel. But they are not without influence over what those feelings become.

Over time, that influence is enough to shape the direction of a life.

The Discipline of Refusal
APRIL 2, 2026 | philosophy notes

As people grow in their self understanding, they become more selective. Not out of bitterness, but out of clarity. People often misread themselves and assume this selectivity is cruelty, when it is actually a form of self-respect.

Why do people think this is malice? The answer is simple: most never question access until it is taken away. What feels like rejection is often just the discipline of refusal.

What is the difference between being bitter and having clarity? Bitterness is reactionary, it punishes others and often clouds judgment. Whilst clarity is deliberate, it withdraws where necessary, not out of anger, but from understanding. To refuse certain people or influences is not to harm, but to preserve your own strength.

Bitterness is parasitic, clarity knows when to let go. Many confuse the two because both result in distance. But distance born from insight carries strength, while distance born from resentment carries weight.

Self-understanding is the foundation of clarity. Without it, boundaries are arbitrary, and selectivity feels cruel rather than wise. The more precisely one knows their values, needs, and limits, the less tolerant they become of what drains or diminishes them. Growth naturally narrows the circle of influence, not out of ego, but out of necessity.

Understanding yourself turns refusal from a reaction into a choice. Every acceptance implies a rejection of something else. In other words, knowing what you truly need allows you to act deliberately, rather than react out of habit, expectation, or pressure.

Rejection is not a failure or a flaw, rather it is a form of self-preservation. Saying "no" protects your time, energy, and sense of self. Without it, even a well meaning relationship or opportunity can start to drain you, diluting your focus and values.

Every boundary you set is an act of self-clarity. To refuse what does not serve you is not cruelty; it is the disciplined maintenance of what sustains you. Growth requires this selectivity, because not everything is meant to belong in your life.

Selectivity often provokes discomfort in others. Society prizes openness, agreeability, and accessibility, so when someone sets firm boundaries, it can be mistaken for arrogance or coldness. People rarely respect the discipline behind refusal; they choose to only see the distance it creates.

This discomfort says more about them than it does about you. We admire authenticity in theory, but resent it in practice when it excludes us. The wiser you become, the more you realize that resistance from others is an inevitable consequence of protecting what elevates you.

Not everything deserves access to you, and not every refusal is a loss. "He who has a why to live can bear almost any how."
